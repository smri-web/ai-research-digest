The Alignment Illusion in Multimodal Large Language Models

Hong-Han Wang, Yuntao Wang, Hu Ding · arXiv

What they studied: Researchers studied how Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) integrate visual and text information.

Why they studied it: They wanted to know if standard measures of visual-text similarity truly reflect how MLLMs process content.

Method: They tested 13 MLLMs by replacing visual input with noise and observing how alignment scores and task accuracy changed.

Findings and results: Standard similarity scores did not consistently show the difference between original and corrupted visual input, a problem they call the alignment illusion. A new measure, the principal-angle gap, tracked task accuracy better.

Benefits and impact: This work clarifies how to interpret internal visual-text alignment in MLLMs, suggesting it is a diagnostic of the visual stream rather than a direct measure of content interaction.

Read the full breakdown

When MLLMs combine images and text, researchers often look at how similar visual and text information become within the model's layers. This similarity is usually seen as proof that the model is blending the visual content into its understanding. However, this paper shows that common ways of measuring this similarity can be misleading. They found that even when visual input was replaced with noise, many standard similarity scores still showed high alignment. This is because the language model's structure can create a false sense of alignment. They propose a new metric, the principal-angle gap, which better reflects when the visual information is actually useful for the model's tasks.

A Living Benchmark for Information Retrieval from Electronic Health Records

Jordan L. Cahoon, Chloe O. Stanwyck, Sulaiman Somani et al. · arXiv

What they studied: The researchers studied how well large language models (LLMs) retrieve information from electronic health records (EHRs). They looked at how these models perform as clinical assistants.

Why they studied it: Current evaluation methods for clinical LLMs are expensive, outdated, and cannot keep up with technology changes. Rigorous evaluation is needed for the safety and usefulness of LLM-based clinical assistants.

Method: They developed a system to automatically create question-answer pairs from EHR notes. Nineteen clinicians validated this system, which produced a benchmark called BRIE.

Findings and results: State-of-the-art LLMs often missed important clinical details. This was especially true for questions needing information from multiple documents or patient visits.

Benefits and impact: This new benchmark allows for continuous, up-to-date evaluation of clinical LLMs. It can also generate multiple answers to reflect different clinician reasoning.

Read the full breakdown

Large language models are being used more often in healthcare to help clinicians find patient information. Evaluating these AI tools is difficult because current tests are expensive to update and quickly become obsolete. Researchers created a new system called BRIE to automatically generate test questions and answers from real patient records. Nineteen clinicians confirmed the quality of this system. When tested, even the best LLMs frequently missed important medical information, particularly when they had to combine details from several parts of a patient's record. This new method provides a way to continuously test and improve clinical LLMs as healthcare technology advances.

How Reproducible Are Evaluation Conclusions? A Self-Audit of LLM-Inferred Prompt Structure

Dipankar Sarkar · arXiv

What they studied: Researchers studied how reproducible evaluation conclusions are for LLM systems, specifically using prompt structure inference as a case study.

Why they studied it: LLM evaluations often use small prompt sets and report model rankings, so the researchers wanted to know how much confidence these rankings deserve.

Method: They analyzed eight open LLM variants from five families, ranging from 8B to 675B parameters, persisting 293 intermediate representations.

Findings and results: Identical calls did not reliably recover identical structure, with node-set Jaccard scores from 0.39 to 0.96. Only the bottom of the model ranking was firm, with the two least reproducible models holding rank in 99% and 86% of replicates. The middle four models held rank in 27% to 48% of replicates, and the top two in 68% each.

Benefits and impact: The study shows that small-sample LLM evaluations can appear more definitive than the evidence supports, highlighting a need for more robust reporting practices.

Read the full breakdown

Evaluations of large language models (LLMs) often present models in a ranked table based on small sets of prompts. This paper questioned the reliability of such rankings, using LLM-based prompt structure inference as a test case. They found that even identical calls to LLMs did not consistently produce the same structure, with node-set Jaccard scores varying widely. When auditing the evaluation, only the lowest-ranked models were consistently identified. The study recommends reporting rank stability, per-cell provenance, sensitivity comparisons, raw outputs, and measurement dates alongside any LLM ranking.
